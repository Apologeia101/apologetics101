Immigration, Borders and Compassion

Immigration is often discussed in moral language: compassion, justice, and care for the vulnerable. Christians rightly care about these things. But compassion must also be guided by wisdom, responsibility, and a realistic understanding of human societies.

Anchor posture

“Seek the peace and prosperity of the city to which I have carried you… Pray to the Lord for it, because if it prospers, you too will prosper.” (Jeremiah 29:7)

1) The common claims

  • “Borders are immoral.”
  • “Christians must support open borders.”
  • “Restricting immigration is unloving.”
  • “Compassion requires accepting anyone who wants to enter.”

These slogans often assume that the only morally acceptable position is maximum openness. But ethical questions about immigration are more complex than this.

2) The biblical balance

The Bible contains two important principles that must be held together.

  • Care for the foreigner and the vulnerable.
  • Responsibility for ordered societies and just governance.

For example, the Old Testament repeatedly commands Israel to treat foreigners with justice and kindness. At the same time, Israel still had defined national borders, laws, and communal identity.

Compassion for outsiders did not eliminate the existence of political communities.

3) Why borders exist

Every functioning society has borders. Borders allow nations to maintain legal systems, democratic accountability, and social stability.

Without borders it becomes impossible for a community to decide:

  • who enters
  • how many can realistically be integrated
  • how resources are distributed

In this sense borders are not necessarily an expression of hostility. They are part of responsible self-government.

4) The Australian context

Australia is one of the most successful immigrant societies in the world. Millions of people have migrated here and built prosperous lives.

At the same time Australia maintains controlled immigration policies, including humanitarian refugee programs and border enforcement.

These policies exist because geography, infrastructure, and social cohesion place practical limits on how quickly societies can absorb new populations.

The ethical question is therefore not simply whether immigration should exist, but how immigration can occur in a way that is both compassionate and sustainable.

5) The challenge of cultural integration

Modern Western societies are built on certain moral foundations:

  • the equal dignity of all people
  • freedom of religion
  • rule of law
  • democratic accountability

Historically these ideas emerged from a mixture of classical philosophy and Judeo-Christian moral teaching.

Most immigrants adopt these values and integrate successfully. However, tensions can arise when ideological or religious systems contain beliefs that conflict with these principles.

For example, debates sometimes arise about the compatibility of certain interpretations of Islamic law with Western commitments to religious freedom, gender equality, and secular government.

Raising these questions does not imply hostility toward Muslims as individuals. Many Muslim migrants integrate successfully and contribute positively to Western societies.

But thoughtful immigration policy must still consider how different worldviews interact with the institutions that sustain free societies.

6) The paradox of compassion

Christians are called to compassion. But compassion can sometimes produce unintended consequences.

Some writers describe a phenomenon called “toxic empathy” — where emotional concern for immediate suffering leads to policies that ultimately harm larger communities or vulnerable people in the long run.

For example, policies that appear compassionate in the short term may unintentionally encourage dangerous migration journeys, human trafficking networks, or overwhelmed public systems.

True compassion must therefore be guided by wisdom. It asks not only what feels kind in the moment, but what genuinely produces human flourishing over time.

7) A Christian approach

A balanced Christian approach to immigration might include several commitments:

  • Welcoming legal immigrants and refugees.
  • Maintaining lawful and humane borders.
  • Encouraging cultural integration and shared civic values.
  • Providing practical support for migrants already living in our communities.

Christians should resist simplistic slogans. Instead we should aim for policies that combine compassion, prudence, and long-term social stability.

Calm responses you can actually use

“Jesus was a refugee, so Christians must support open borders.”

Caring for refugees is clearly a Christian responsibility. But the Bible never teaches that nations cannot have borders or immigration laws.

“Restricting immigration is unloving.”

Love involves responsibility for neighbours and communities as well as newcomers. Wise immigration policy tries to protect both.

“Borders are immoral.”

Every functioning society in history has had borders. The moral question is how those borders are administered.
